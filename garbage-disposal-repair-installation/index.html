Before you call: two safe checks

  1. Switch it off at the wall

    Never put your hand in the disposal, even when it looks off.

  2. Use the hex wrench slot

    Most disposals have a socket at the bottom. A hex wrench turned back and forth frees a jam.

  3. Press the red reset button

    It sits on the bottom of the unit. A tripped reset means the motor overheated.

  4. Call if it still hums or leaks

    A motor that hums but does not turn or a leak at a seal usually needs repair or replacement.

What you should not put down a disposal

Grease, coffee grounds in volume, pasta and rice, celery and potato peels can all clog or jam a disposal and the drain after it. Fibrous and starchy foods swell and settle in the trap. Run cold water for 15 to 30 seconds after use and run it with a few ice cubes now and then to knock buildup off the blades.

If the sink drains slowly even with the disposal running, the clog may sit further down the kitchen line. Standing water in a double sink, or water that rises in the second basin when the disposal runs, points the same way. See drain cleaning.

Choosing a replacement disposal

Horsepower matters for households that cook a lot. A 1/3 or 1/2 horsepower unit suits light use. A 3/4 or 1 horsepower unit grinds tougher scraps and runs quieter. Continuous-feed units are the most common and batch-feed units run only when the stopper is turned.

We confirm that the sink flange, the drain outlet and the dishwasher connection suit the model you choose. A dishwasher connected to the disposal needs the knockout plug removed, and forgetting it makes the dishwasher drain back into the sink. It is a small step we make sure to do.

Standards behind the work

Disposal drains connect to the kitchen trap under the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC). According to the City of Milpitas Office of Building Safety, a like-for-like swap is a repair, while moving drain lines is permit work.
