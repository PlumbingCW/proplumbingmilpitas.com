How to Plumb a Shower Base: A Milpitas, CA Homeowner Guide

A new shower base is one of the most common bathroom projects, and the plumbing under it decides whether it leaks. The base itself sits on top of a drain, a trap and a vent, and the work must be done in the right order and inspected before the floor closes up. This guide walks through the plumbing steps and shows where Milpitas rules apply.

Know what kind of base you have

There are two common types. A prefabricated base is a one-piece acrylic or fiberglass pan that sits on a mortar bed or directly on the subfloor, with a drain opening already formed. A site-built base is a tiled floor over a waterproof liner, sloped to a drain by the installer. The plumbing underneath is similar, but the way the drain connects to the pan is different.

Prefabricated bases are faster and have fewer leak points, which is why they are common in remodels. Site-built bases suit custom sizes but rely on a perfect liner. Decide which you want before the plumbing starts, because the drain height and the floor opening depend on the base.

The plumbing steps in order

  1. Confirm the drain location

    Mark the center of the drain on the subfloor from the base's documentation. The drain must land within the opening of the pan.

  2. Set the trap and the drain pipe

    A shower drain needs a trap, usually a P-trap, connected to a 2 inch drain line. The trap holds water that blocks sewer gas.

  3. Connect the vent

    The trap needs a vent to drain freely. The vent often ties into an existing vent in the wall.

  4. Pressure test or water test the drain

    Fill the line with water or air to check every joint before anything is covered.

  5. Install the shower valve and supply lines

    The valve is set at the right depth for the finished wall. Hot and cold lines connect to the valve body.

  6. Set the base

    Follow the manufacturer's instructions for the mortar bed or the subfloor support, and connect the base drain to the trap.

  7. Flood test

    Plug the drain and fill the base with water for several hours. Mark the water level and check for a drop.

  8. Rough inspection, then close up

    Do not cover any plumbing until the city inspector has seen it.

Why the trap and vent matter more than the base

Many shower leaks and slow drains start under the floor, not in the pan. A trap set too low, a trap without a vent or a drain line without slope will drain slowly and gurgle. Shower drains are usually 2 inches, and the line must slope toward the main drain at about a quarter inch per foot.

A properly vented trap drains with a smooth swirl. An unvented trap can lock with air and pull the water out of the trap, which lets sewer gas up. If your remodel adds a shower where none existed, new vent routing may be needed. This is one reason a plumbing permit and a rough inspection exist.

Common mistakes that lead to leaks

  • Skipping the flood testA pan can look watertight and still leak at the drain connection. A flood test shows it.
  • Wrong liner folds on a site-built panA liner folded at the drain flange can trap water in the wrong place.
  • Trap too far from the drainLong distances between the drain and the trap slow the flow and collect scum.
  • No access panelWithout an access panel behind the valve, a repair means breaking the wall. See plumbing installation.
  • Valve set at the wrong depthThe valve trim does not seat against the tile.
  • Covering work before inspectionThe inspector may require you to open the wall again.

Local note

Permits in Milpitas for a new shower

The City of Milpitas says installing or replacing plumbing fixtures and piping in a building requires a plumbing permit, and that extensive removal of wall or ceiling structure also requires a building permit. A remodel that moves a shower, adds a drain or changes the vent falls into that.

The city's handout expects a rough inspection before walls or ceilings are covered and a final inspection when the work is done.

Water piping to the shower valve must hold pressure for at least 15 minutes in the city's test, and drain and vent lines are also inspected. Milpitas homes from the 1950s and 1960s often have a slab or a raised foundation with limited access from below. A new shower in a slab house may need the slab opened to reach the drain, which adds a repair step.

If your house is in North San Jose rather than Milpitas, the City of San Jose runs its own permits, and our San Jose service page has the details.

When to hire a plumber instead of doing it yourself

Some parts are within reach of a careful homeowner, such as setting a prefabricated base on a mortar bed or installing trim. The drain, trap and vent are different, because mistakes hide under the floor and surface later as stains or smells. If you are moving a drain, tying into an existing vent or cutting a slab, a plumber is the safer choice.

We rough in the plumbing, test it, schedule the inspection and leave the walls open until the inspector signs off. If the new shower is part of a bigger remodel, we can also check the old pipe and suggest a repipe while the walls are open.
