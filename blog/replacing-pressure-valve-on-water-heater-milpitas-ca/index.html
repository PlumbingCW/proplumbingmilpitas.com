Replacing a Water Heater Pressure Valve: Milpitas, CA Homeowner Guide

The pressure relief valve on a water heater is a small brass fitting with a lever and a pipe running down from it. It is the part that stops a tank from becoming a pressure vessel with nowhere to go.

If it drips, hisses or has never been touched in ten years, it is worth a look. This guide explains how the valve works, how to decide whether to replace it and how the job is done.

What the valve actually does

The valve is called a temperature and pressure relief valve, or T&P valve. It opens on its own when the water in the tank gets too hot or the pressure climbs too high. A spring holds it shut. When pressure or temperature passes its set point, the spring gives and water flows out through the discharge pipe, which relieves the pressure and prevents the tank from failing.

Most residential valves are rated to open near 150 psi or 210 degrees Fahrenheit. Those numbers are printed on the valve. The rating must match the tank, so a replacement valve is bought for the tank, not just for the thread size. If you are not sure, the label on the water heater lists the working pressure and the manufacturer's data plate lists the valve rating.

Signs the valve needs replacing

Constant dripping at the discharge pipe

A valve that drips often has a worn seat or mineral buildup. It may also be telling you the pressure in the house is too high.

Valve is corroded or crusted

White or green crust around the valve body means water has been weeping for a long time.

Lever stuck or loose

The lever should lift and drop with a click. If it will not move, the valve may not open when it matters.

Valve is older than the tank warranty

Many plumbers swap the valve whenever the tank is replaced or after about ten years.

Discharge when the heater cycles

A burst of water each time the heater fires can mean thermal expansion. Fix that first, because a new valve will do the same.

No discharge pipe at all

The valve must be piped down to a safe place. A missing pipe is a code problem as well as a safety one.

Check the cause before you swap the valve

Replacing the valve does not cure the reason it opened. Three causes are common. The first is a worn valve that leaks at normal pressure. The second is thermal expansion in a closed system, where hot water has no place to go and pushes the pressure up each heating cycle. The third is high incoming pressure from a failed regulator.

A pressure gauge on a hose bib shows the incoming pressure in a minute. The California Plumbing Code material the City of Milpitas hands out calls for a pressure regulator where pressure exceeds 80 psi. If your gauge reads higher, the regulator is the real problem.

If the pressure is normal but the valve drips after each heating cycle, an expansion tank is usually the cure. A plumber can tell you which in one visit, and the answer decides whether a new valve alone is enough.

How the replacement is done

  1. Shut off the fuel and the water

    Turn the gas control to pilot or off, or switch off the breaker for an electric heater. Close the cold water valve above the tank.

  2. Relieve pressure and drain a little

    Open a hot tap in the house and lift the valve lever to let air in. Drain a few gallons through the drain valve so the water level drops below the valve port.

  3. Remove the old valve and the discharge pipe

    Unscrew the pipe, then turn the valve out with a wrench. Note how far it sits in the tank.

  4. Install the new valve

    Wrap thread seal tape on the threads, thread the valve in by hand and snug it with a wrench. Do not overtighten.

  5. Reconnect the discharge pipe

    The pipe must run downhill, be as large as the valve outlet and end in a safe place. It must not be capped or threaded at the end.

  6. Refill and test

    Close the drain valve, open the cold supply, let the tank fill with a hot tap open, then restore fuel or power. Check for leaks and lift the lever once to confirm it seats again.

Mistakes that cause trouble later

  • Capping the discharge pipeIt defeats the purpose of the valve. The pipe must stay open.
  • Using a valve with the wrong ratingA valve rated too high may not open when it should. Match the tank rating.
  • Reusing the old discharge fittingsA rusty fitting can fail the next time the valve opens.
  • Leaving the heater on during the swapAn electric element running dry burns out fast. Switch the breaker off.
  • Ignoring the water pressureA new valve on a house with 100 psi will also drip.
Local note

What the City of Milpitas says about water heaters

The City of Milpitas Office of Building Safety lists water heater work in its plumbing permit guidance. Replacing a water heater needs a plumbing permit. The city's handout also expects a temperature and pressure relief valve on the tank, with a discharge pipe that ends in a safe location, and it calls for seismic strapping and a seismic gas shutoff where gas is used.

A relief valve swap on its own is a repair of an existing appliance, but a new tank, a new location or a changed vent is new work.

If your valve drips and the tank is already 10 to 12 years old, the drip may be a sign the tank is near the end. See water heater repair to compare a repair against water heater replacement.

After the swap: how to keep the valve healthy

Lift the lever once a year to confirm it moves freely and water flows out. Have a bucket or a funnel ready, because it may spray briefly. If it does not reseat and keeps dripping, the seat has debris on it. Lifting the lever a few more times sometimes clears it. If it still drips, the valve needs replacing again.

Flush the tank once a year to remove sediment. Milpitas water ranges from soft to fairly hard through the year, and the minerals settle at the bottom of the tank and shorten its life. Sediment also makes the tank run hotter at the bottom, which stresses the valve. If you see white crust forming on fittings, a water softener or a scale filter may help.
